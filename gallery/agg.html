Near misses that add up

Lognormal risks eXj, with X Gaussian, are split into groups, and every group total must exceed a high level u. Within a single group, a large total comes from its largest risk: the single-big-jump principle. Across groups this can fail. The paper shows that P(all group sums > u) ∼ C* P(all group maxima > u) as u → ∞, with a constant C* ≥ 1 determined by the cheapest Gaussian configurations. Here group 1 is one risk eX1, and group 2 has two risks with X2 = X1 + cW2 and X3 = X1 + cW3, where (W2, W3) is standard Gaussian with correlation r, independent of X1. The cheapest way to make both groups large is a single jump X1 > log u. It also lifts both risks of group 2 to the level u: given the jump, eX2/u and eX3/u converge in law to ecW2 and ecW3. Group 2 then exceeds u when one of its risks does, or when two near misses add up, so C* = P(ecW2 + ecW3 > 1) / P(max(W2, W3) > 0) > 1. In general, C* > 1 exactly when such a boundary group contains at least two risks. With c = 1 and r = 0 this is Example 3.1 of the paper. In Example 3.2, three groups of two risks from a stationary Gaussian process, the middle group is the boundary group, with c = 1/√2 and r = 1/2. From E. Hashorva, Aggregating lognormal risks in groups: joint tails and copulas.

The probabilities are limits as u → ∞, conditional on the jump in group 1; this jump is the dominant configuration of Theorem 2.1 (index set I = {1}, with group 2 as its boundary group). P(max(W2, W3) > 0) = 3/4 − arcsin(r)/(2π) is exact. P(ecW2 + ecW3 > 1) is a one-dimensional Gaussian integral, computed by Simpson's rule on 400 intervals; it reproduces the paper's values C* ≈ 1.182066 and 1.332802. Nothing is simulated.
