Ruin in every block, ruin in every window

The loss process X(t) = B(t) − ct of a Brownian risk model is watched over a horizon T = mh cut into m reporting blocks of length h. Block ruin asks for an exceedance of the level u in every block; sliding-window ruin asks for one in every window of length h, wherever it is placed — equivalently, no recovery interval of length h. Both are far rarer than ordinary ruin on [0, T], but not by a product of one-block probabilities. Conditional on ruin in the first block, the first hit of u happens just before h; the strong Markov property there turns everything that follows into a finite-level event, so pbl ∼ 2 Gm Ψ((u+ch)/√h) and psl ∼ 2 Gslm Ψ((u+ch)/√h), where Gm is the probability that a fresh drifted Brownian motion is positive somewhere in each of the blocks [kh,(k+1)h], k = 1,…,m−2, and Gslm the Parisian-type survival probability that it never stays non-positive for a whole window. The animation rains down Brownian paths until one achieves the event, then replays it. From Dębicki, Hashorva & Michna, Finite-horizon block and sliding-window ruin for Brownian risk models.

Euler step h/200. The constants Gm, Gslm and the grid constant Jm = P{B(jh) − cjh > 0, j ≤ m−2} are themselves Monte Carlo estimates (4 000 paths, no rare event involved); the chain Jm ≤ Gslm ≤ Gm ≤ 1 is the paper's (10). Discrete suprema slightly undercount crossings, which lowers every empirical frequency a little. Brute-force search becomes slow once ψ(u,h) drops below about 10−5; the panel says so.
